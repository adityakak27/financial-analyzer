Executive summary

The market pays for fundamental quality, but only a little, and a "cheap for its quality" signal does not predict returns. A 0-100 Fundamental Quality Score built from SEC 10-K fundamentals correlates with valuation at r=0.37 and explains 14.1% of the spread in EV/Sales. Tested out of sample -- each company judged against its own sub-industry, with a fair-value line fitted only on earlier years, and returns measured against the XLK tech ETF -- "undervalued" companies did not beat "overvalued" ones. The classic risk scores do not predict excess returns, and management tone describes the past rather than the future. Every number is computed by deterministic code; the AI layer (Gemini) only narrates verified outputs, and each of its figures is checked against the data before publication.

Research questions

  • RQ1. Do higher-quality companies trade at higher valuation multiples, and how much of valuation does quality explain?
  • RQ2. Which companies look undervalued or overvalued for their quality, does a DCF agree, and did the verdicts predict returns?
  • RQ3. Did companies with better Piotroski F, Altman Z or Beneish M scores have better next-year growth and returns?
  • RQ4. Does MD&A sentiment match actual performance, in the same year and the next?
  • RQ5. Do semiconductors, software and hardware differ in quality, valuation and the link between them?

Data and sample

  • Companies: 57 -- the 19-ticker FinChat universe plus 38 S&P 500 Information Technology constituents with a full FY2018-2024 10-K history. ANSYS and Juniper Networks were dropped: both were acquired and no longer appear in SEC's active ticker map (a survivorship bias -- see Limitations).
  • Sources: SEC EDGAR XBRL company facts (statements, ratios, Piotroski and Beneish scores), SEC 10-K MD&A text (sentiment), Yahoo Finance via yfinance (daily adjusted closes for each company and for the XLK Technology Select Sector ETF, cached locally).

Method

  • Quality Score (0-100) from raw fundamentals only, in four pillars: Profitability (ROE, ROIC, operating margin, net margin), Growth (revenue and net-income growth), Safety (current ratio, debt/equity, interest coverage) and Earnings Quality (FCF margin, accruals). Each metric is z-scored within its fiscal year (sign-flipped where lower is better), averaged into pillars (a pillar needs at least 2 of its metrics), averaged into one score and rescaled to 0-100. Altman Z, Piotroski F and Beneish M are not inputs: they are composites of the same ratios, and Altman Z contains market capitalisation, which would place market valuation inside a score that is regressed on valuation. The three scores are tested separately in RQ3.
  • Valuation: EV/Sales (defined even at a loss), log-transformed for regression; P/E is missing when EPS <= 0. EV/Sales, log EV/Sales, P/E, ROE, ROIC and debt/equity are winsorized at the 5th/95th percentile within each fiscal year.
  • Point-in-time prices: the close on the first trading day on or after each 10-K filing date (at most 7 calendar days later) sets market cap and EV; next-12-month returns run from that day. Altman Z is recomputed from that point-in-time market cap. The pre-existing scores table reused each company's current market cap for every historical year (NVDA's FY2018 Altman Z was 486), which this study does not use.
  • Returns are benchmark-adjusted: each stock's 12-month total return minus XLK's total return over the same two trading days.
  • RQ1 regressions are descriptive and in-sample: simple OLS of log(EV/Sales) on Quality Score, and a controlled OLS adding revenue growth, fiscal-year and sub-industry fixed effects, with standard errors clustered by ticker.
  • RQ2 verdicts are walk-forward and sub-industry-relative: for fiscal year t, each sub-industry's fair-value line is fitted only on that sub-industry's company-years from years before t. A company's gap is its actual minus expected log(EV/Sales); gaps are standardised across all companies within year t, and z <= -1 is "Undervalued", z >= +1 "Overvalued". FY2018 has no earlier data, so verdicts start in FY2019.
  • DCF: finchat/engine/dcf.py with default assumptions (9% WACC, 2.5% terminal growth, 5 years), run as of each historical fiscal year via a backward-compatible as_of_fy parameter.
  • Grouped tests (RQ3): strong vs weak groups -- Piotroski 7-9 vs 0-3, Altman safe (>3.0) vs distress (<1.81), Beneish not flagged vs flagged (> -1.78) -- compared with Welch's t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test.

Results

RQ1 -- Quality vs valuation

Model Slope on Quality Score p-value R² N
Simple (QualityScore only) 0.026 <0.001 0.141 383
With controls (revenue growth, year FE, sub-industry FE; clustered SE) 0.0222 0.003 0.323 330

Quality and valuation are positively correlated (Pearson r=0.37, p<0.001, N=383). On its own the Quality Score explains 14.1% of the variation in log(EV/Sales). Adding revenue growth, year and sub-industry effects raises R² to 32.3% and the sample falls from 383 to 330 company-years (revenue growth needs a prior year); the quality coefficient stays significant (p=0.003). Most of the added explanatory power comes from the year and sub-industry effects -- tech multiples rose sharply into FY2023-2024 for every quality level, and software trades richer than hardware at the same quality -- not from quality itself. The yearly correlation rises from r=0.18 in FY2018 to r=0.54 in FY2024.

How the AI system uses these results

The study above is the evidence layer of FinChat; this section is how the AI layer turns it into answers without ever doing the maths itself.

  1. Computation and narration are separate. Every figure -- Quality Scores, fair-value lines, verdicts, DCF, grouped outcomes -- is computed by deterministic Python (finchat/engine/mispricing.py) and stored in SQLite. The language model never calculates; it only reads tool output.
  2. Tool use. The Gemini agent chooses among 15 tools, three of them added for this study: get_valuation_check (one company's score, pillars, verdict, fair-value line and DCF), list_mispriced (leaderboards) and get_score_effectiveness (RQ3 outcomes). Every tool returns JSON with a source field, so each answer can be traced to a table.
  3. Number grounding. Every number in AI-written text must match a value in the data it was given (within 1%, or x100 for percentages); notation such as R^2 or FY2024 is ignored. A failing draft is regenerated once, then replaced by a deterministic template, so nothing unverified is published. For this report Gemini wrote 21 of 21 texts; 1 drafts were rejected by the check and regenerated, and 21 of 21 published texts are verified.
  4. Guardrails in the system prompt: a verdict is a statistical comparison, not a recommendation; always state the fair-value line's R²; say the verdicts did not predict returns in back-tests; never add causes the tools did not state; end with "Not investment advice".

Key findings

An analysis of 57 companies across 383 company-years spanning FY2018-FY2024 reveals that fundamental quality shares a real but modest relationship with market valuation. The correlation between fundamental quality and valuation yields an r of 0.37, with an R² of 0.141, indicating that fundamental quality explains 14.1% of the variation in valuation. Furthermore, quality remains significant even when applying controls, yielding a p of 0.003. When testing out-of-sample verdicts for FY2019-FY2024, the data identified 12 companies categorized as undervalued in the latest year and 8 companies categorized as overvalued in the latest year. However, these verdicts did not successfully predict subsequent performance, as the undervalued group registered an average excess return versus XLK of -3.0%, while the overvalued group recorded an average excess return versus XLK of 1.1%, with a difference yielding a p of 0.64.

Additional fundamental tests produced varied outcomes across the sample. The Piotroski strong versus weak excess return test yielded a p of 0.32, and the Beneish clean versus flagged excess return test resulted in a p of 0.26. The Altman safe versus distress excess return test showed a p of 1.0. However, the Altman test highlighted distinct operational differences, with safe companies achieving a next-year revenue growth of 11.6% compared to 5.7% for distressed companies, a statistically significant difference with a p of 0.006.

Finally, textual analysis of management tone demonstrated divergent relationships with growth and performance metrics. Management tone showed a correlation with same-year revenue growth of r = 0.32, but exhibited virtually no correlation with next 12-month excess returns, recording an r of -0.01. The data suggests that fundamental metrics relate to operational results and baseline valuations, but do not reliably track subsequent excess returns in this sample. Not investment advice.

Limitations

  • Small, single-sector sample. 57 companies and 399 company-years; RQ2's out-of-sample test has 108 flagged company-years. Findings describe large US tech only.
  • Survivorship bias. Companies were chosen from today's index; acquired or delisted firms (ANSYS, Juniper) are missing, which tilts the sample toward survivors.
  • Staggered fiscal years. Z-scores and winsorization are computed within a fiscal-year label, but year-ends range from late January (NVDA) to December (most companies), so a January filer is ranked against peers whose same-labelled year closes up to eleven months later. Prices and fair-value lines are point-in-time; this cross-sectional ranking is not.
  • Coarse sub-industries. "Software & Services" mixes high-multiple software with low-multiple IT services (DXC, CTSH, ACN, EPAM, CDW), so some IT-services "undervalued" verdicts reflect business mix.
  • EV/Sales ignores margins. A 5%-margin assembler and a 40%-margin software firm can look alike on quality yet deserve very different sales multiples; EV/EBIT or EV/gross profit would control for this.
  • Non-random missing data. Beneish M needs SG&A, PP&E and a prior year, so coverage is 14% in Software & Services versus 67% in Semiconductors; Beneish results say little about software.
  • The DCF is a uniform yardstick: one WACC (9%) and terminal growth (2.5%) for every company, no CAPM beta, and free cash flow is not reduced by stock-based compensation, which flatters tech companies.
  • Score design. Altman Z was built on 1960s manufacturers and saturates for asset-light firms; the Loughran-McDonald word counts ignore negation and boilerplate.
  • Correlation, not causation. RQ1 is descriptive; quality and valuation may both reflect growth expectations.

Conclusion

Fundamental quality and valuation are positively related in this tech sample, but modestly: quality alone explains about 14% of the spread in EV/Sales, and sector and timing explain more. When the "cheap for its quality" signal is tested properly -- each company against its own sub-industry, with a fair-value line that never sees the future, and returns measured against the tech sector -- it does not predict next-year returns. The classic risk scores do not predict excess returns either, although Altman Z does flag weaker next-year revenue growth and more losses, and management tone describes the past rather than the future. The honest conclusion is that a quality score built from public filings is a useful way to describe and screen companies, but in large-cap tech it is not, on its own, a source of returns -- which is why every Radar verdict is labelled "Not investment advice".
